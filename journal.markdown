/notes on raising a deeptech seed/
from a non-consensus deeply ambitious unorthodox team on the US-India corridor

Everyone loves giving fundraising advice. There is something addictive about the posture involved in telling founders how to raise money. Yet, most fundraising advice is not useful to the average non-Bay non-AI founder. This is because it generally comes from
a) VCs. There is some value in knowing how some of them think but most are too disconnected from what it actually takes to build, especially at the earliest stages
b) YC founders. As of 2026, YC is mainstream, has pedigree and are/shape consensus, so this advice is useless for most founders operating out of those straitjackets
c) Second-time founders. Completely different game than first-time founders and generally don't remember what it was like the first time around
d) Bay area founders.

The most important advice is to have solid days. Being psychologically strong is probably the biggest determinant of fundraising success, everything else is downstream. 

Takes longer than you think. Two week party sprint is 2021 SaaS and 2026 Agents. Work backwards from 12mos of running out of runway. This is when you start planning your raise. Figure out the right season where you will still have 6mos+ runway. E.g. if runway runs out in March 2027, you want to start in the Fall season around Aug-Sept 2026. Don't underweight the importance of seasons, especially if you're not American. Raising 3 weeks before Thanksgiving is a bad idea (trust me) and timing the launch of your raise poorly will be the single biggest variable of its success.

You then want to start actively devoting time to fundraising beginning Feb-March 2026. This mainly looks like connecting with other founders, especially those whose last raised round is the same one you're looking to raise. If you're raising a seed, it's not a bad idea to also reach out to founders who have shot past a Series C, but they may be less useful in their insight and intros than a founder who just raised a strong seed 9mos ago. See founders as sensors of VC. You generally want to connect with founders as founders, and not as a means to intros. This can be hard, especially if the founders in question are in the Bay and you're in Bangalore and there are no obvious ways to connect/collaborate other than the intro. There is no perfect/ideal way to do this, honesty and transparency is generally appreciated, as well as being mindful that founders tend to be busier than your average VC and its not their job to look out for dealflow. The more valuable a founder's rolodex of intros, the busier they likely are. Make it as easy as possible for them to help you. Send forwardables and tailored blurbs. Follow-up proactively and politely, though don't hound them if they have displayed hesitation. A warm intro is not "free", the founder often expends social capital by the exercise of taste. At the same time, if there a strong fit + they understand what you do and like it, it can be win-win to provide the intro.

Get the story down. Takes longer than you think. See Khosla's nailing your raise. Each slide should have a single message (that conveys fear or greed.) Assume people will spend less than 5s per slide. Get a freelance designer to iterate with you, polishing up versions. Don't underestimate good design but definitely don't overestimate it's ability to patch over a weak story.

Plenty of advice on what good decks look like. Contingent on market, VC, thesis, sector. Important as your top of funnel but it will rarely be what allows you to close. Tailor it accordingly. Craft something that gets your the first call, and then nail your pitch to get the second call. 

Build a FAQ for people to look at when doing diligence. Build an appendix to answer questions on a call. Build a data room for people to dig in further. If you're worried about people sharing your material with competitors, use DocSend. If you're not worried, still use Docsend but also consider sharing a zip folder so the analyst/principal/partner can play with your material in their LLM of choice. Try this yourself, use an account without any context on your company and put in the dataroom with a typical prompt a VC may use. This is useful feedback to make your dataroom stronger.

Do the usual things of making a spreadsheet or control room or whatever on funds you think are a fit, intro pathways to get there, check size, etc etc. Build out a rolodex of warm intros to as many funds as possible. This can come from your current investors, friends/advisors, fellow founders etc. See the tyranny of warm intros. Cold emailing/DMing is worth a shot but has a very low chance of success. Same with decks on websites. You can try it, and can optimize for it (get an agent to do it) but it likely is more useful to find the right intro.

An underrated but tricky play is to make a concerted PR push to generate organic inbound. This can look like founders posting a lot on socials, earned media, or announcing your last fundraise in a big way. The reason its tricky is that it takes meaningful effort/attention away from building and it is not trivial to get right. But the value of investors reaching out to you vs you reaching out to them cannot be understated.

Ultimately, remember that rounds raised before Series A (generally the first priced round nowadays) are significantly based on pure vibes. Substance matters but not as much as you think. Signalling, playing round dynamics etc matters alot more than most first-time founders expect. What this concretely means
a) Backchannel as much as possible. Ask your current investors if they ppl art the fund. If you are friends with founders who raised from the fund, this is ideal
b) Be careful about how you signal interest from other funds. VCs have a much richer training set than you on what signals generally mean, and often see through weak attempts
